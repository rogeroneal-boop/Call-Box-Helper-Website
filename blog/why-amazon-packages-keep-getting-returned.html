The Real Reason Your Amazon Packages Keep Getting Returned

You ordered something, tracked it to your city, and then got that notification — "Delivery attempted. We'll try again tomorrow." Or worse, "Package returned to sender." If this keeps happening and you live in an apartment or gated community, the problem almost certainly isn't the driver. Here's what's actually going on.

The delivery attempted notification explained

When Amazon marks a delivery as "attempted," it means the driver showed up but couldn't complete the delivery. For most apartment and gated community residents, this comes down to one of two scenarios:

  • The driver couldn't get through the gate
  • Nobody answered the door after the driver got inside

If you're consistently getting "delivery attempted" notifications without ever hearing a knock or a buzz, the gate is almost always the culprit.

How your gate is blocking your deliveries

If your building uses a call box system, every delivery requires you to actively answer a phone call and press a key to open the gate. The call box dials your number, your phone rings, and you have roughly 20-30 seconds to respond before the driver moves on.

Amazon drivers are on tight schedules. They're optimized for speed — sometimes delivering hundreds of packages per shift. Waiting more than 30 seconds at a gate costs them time they don't have. If you don't answer, they mark it as attempted and leave.

The package then goes one of three places:

  • Back to the delivery van for another attempt the next day
  • To an Amazon locker or pickup location nearby
  • Returned to the sender after multiple failed attempts

The frustrating part: You were probably home. The driver showed up while your phone was on silent, you were in the shower, or you simply didn't recognize the call box number. The delivery failed not because you weren't there — but because you didn't answer a phone call in time.

Why delivery instructions don't solve the problem

The first thing most people try is adding delivery instructions to their Amazon account — "call box code is 9," "buzz unit 204," "leave at gate." Here's why this rarely works for call box buildings:

Call boxes don't accept instructions — they dial phones

A gate code is something a driver can type in and the gate opens. A call box is different — it dials your phone and waits for you to answer. There's no instruction you can give a driver that lets them bypass the need for you to pick up. The call box has to call you, and you have to respond.

Drivers don't always read delivery notes

Amazon drivers are working fast. Delivery notes help but aren't guaranteed to be read, especially during peak hours when drivers are under time pressure.

Instructions don't carry across carriers

What you add to your Amazon account only helps Amazon drivers. UPS, FedEx, USPS, and other carriers won't see your Amazon delivery notes. You'd have to update instructions on every platform separately.

The "leave at gate" problem

Some drivers will leave packages at the gate entrance rather than return them. This seems helpful but creates a different problem — packages left outside a locked gate are visible to anyone walking by and frequently get stolen.

Package theft at gate entrances is common and underreported. The gate was supposed to keep your packages safe, but if the driver can't get through it, your package ends up in the least secure spot possible.

Why this keeps happening repeatedly

Once you've had one failed delivery, you're likely to have more. Here's why:

  • Delivery windows are unpredictable. Amazon gives you a window but actual arrival times vary. You can't be available every moment of a four-hour window.
  • You can't keep your phone on full volume all day. Meetings, workouts, sleep — there will always be times when you miss a call.
  • The call box number looks like spam. Call boxes dial from their own number, which you probably don't have saved. Unknown numbers get ignored, especially with spam calls so common.
  • Same driver, same gate, same problem. If you're on a regular delivery route, the same driver will hit your gate repeatedly with the same result.

The pattern: Delivery attempted → rescheduled → attempted again → you miss it again → returned to sender. If this cycle sounds familiar, the gate is the root cause.

What about Amazon Key?

Amazon Key is Amazon's own solution to delivery access — it works with compatible smart locks and garage door openers to let drivers deliver inside your home or garage. It's a great solution if you own your home and have compatible hardware.

  • Driver marks as "delivery attempted" and moves on
  • Package gets rescheduled, left at gate, or returned to sender
  • Delivery instructions don't help — call boxes require you to answer
